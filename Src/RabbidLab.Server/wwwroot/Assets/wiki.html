Rabbid Lab runs on one machine (the server) and everyone uses it in a browser. Put it on a machine that stays on.

On Windows

  1. Download RabbidLab-Setup-<version>.exe from the latest release on GitHub.
  2. Run it and say yes to the admin prompt.
  3. Pick the folder and shortcuts, then the address (port 8420 unless you change it), then the HTTPS certificate. Rabbid Lab can make its own, or use one you already have.
  4. Click Install. Rabbid Lab installs as a Windows service and starts straight away.
  5. Open https://localhost:8420 on the server, or https://<server name>:8420 from another computer.

Running Setup again updates Rabbid Lab and keeps its settings.

In Docker

  1. Run docker compose up -d in the repository folder.
  2. Open http://<host>:8420. Everything Rabbid Lab keeps goes in the rabbidlab volume.

Next: set it up.

The first time anyone opens Rabbid Lab, it asks how people sign in. Do this right after installing: until it's done, anyone who can reach the port can do it.

With Active Directory

  1. Pick Active Directory.
  2. Check the domain, and leave the domain controller on Automatic unless you want a particular one.
  3. Sign in with your own domain account. You become an Administrator, and everyone in the domain can sign in as a User.

With a local account

  1. Pick Local account.
  2. Make a username and password. That account is the first Administrator.

Who gets what is up to you afterwards, under Roles.

Everything is in the rail down the left. You only see what your roles allow.

  • Lab: Overview and Links, the sites in your lab.
  • Active Directory: Users, Groups and Computers in your domain.
  • Config: Devices (the computers with the client, their Windows accounts and who is an administrator of each), and Roles and people.
  • Help: this wiki, What's new, and GitHub.
  • Settings at the bottom: updates and the activity log.

Your name in the top right shows how you signed in and has Sign out. Collapse at the bottom shrinks the rail to icons.

Computers on Devices, users, groups and computers in Active Directory, and GPOs each have a Notes tab in their side panel. Its number says how many notes there are.

  • Type in the box, then click Add note or press Ctrl+Enter. The toolbar does bold, italic, underline, strikethrough, headings, quotes, lists, code and links (Ctrl+K). Pasted text comes in plain.
  • Everyone who can see the computer, account or GPO reads its notes and can add one. Notes stay on an AD account when it's moved or renamed.
  • The pencil changes your own notes. The bin deletes your own, and whoever can change that kind of thing can delete anyone's.
  • Adding, changing and deleting notes is in the activity log.

A computer shows up under Devices once it has the Rabbid Lab client. There's no key: the client only needs the server's address. New computers wait until an admin approves them.

From the computer itself (easiest)

  1. On that computer, open Rabbid Lab in a browser and sign in.
  2. The bar at the top says it isn't connected yet. Click Download the client and run the MSI.
  3. Click Connect this computer. The client opens with this server's address filled in.
  4. Check the address, click Connect, and choose Yes at the Windows administrator prompt.
  5. Approve it under Devices.

Quietly, from a command line or Intune

  1. Open Devices and click New in the bar under the banner, or pick Add computers from its arrow.
  2. Copy the install line. It has this server's address and its public key fingerprint, which is how the client knows the server.
  3. Run it on the computer, or paste it into Intune as the install command.

With Group Policy, in one click

  1. Open Devices, Deploy the client. It takes Add computers and Change Group Policy, and Group Policy Management on this server.
  2. Pick the address every computer connects to. A full name, such as helpdesk.yourdomain.com, works anywhere in the domain.
  3. Click Deploy to every computer and confirm it's you. Rabbid Lab makes the GPO Rabbid Lab Client, puts the installer in its SYSVOL folder, adds a task that installs it quietly as SYSTEM where it isn't installed, lets the client's file past Defender's attack surface reduction rules, and links the GPO to the domain.
  4. Each computer installs it at its next policy refresh, within about 90 minutes, with no restart. People at the computer see nothing: no install, no tray icon and no messages (the installer is given QUIET=1). New ones wait for approval under Computers.

Deploy again puts this server's installer in the GPO after an update. Stop deploying takes the GPO off the domain; computers keep the client. If Intune manages Defender, the page shows the exclusion to add to your attack surface reduction policy there too.

With your own GPO

In Add computers, download the MSI and the Group Policy template. Assign the MSI under Software Installation, and set Server address under Administrative Templates, Rabbid Lab, Client.

Group Policy wins over anything the installer or Connect was given.

  1. Open Devices. New computers show as Waiting.
  2. Click Approve next to the computer. It's connected from then on.
  3. To remove a computer, click it, then Remove from Devices in the panel on the right. If the client is still installed on it, it connects again and waits for approval.

Don't want to approve each one? In Add computers, turn off Require approval for new computers and they connect right away.

The views under Computers on the left (Online, Offline, Waiting for approval and the rest) narrow the list.

Each computer's client reports what's on it: model, serial number, processor, memory, drives, uptime, whether Windows is waiting for a restart, the installed software, and the Windows updates installed and missing. Nothing is installed or changed on the computer for this.

  • Computers shows the short version on each row. The views on the left pick out what needs looking at: Needs updates, Needs restart, Low disk space (under 15% free on C:) and Older client. More columns are under Columns.
  • Click a computer for its hardware, and tabs with its software and its Windows updates by name. To list software across every computer, run a WMI query.
  • Export saves the open list as a CSV file.

The client looks every half hour and sends when something changed. It asks Windows Update what's missing every six hours; antivirus definition updates are left out. A computer needs client 1.1.165 or later.

On Devices you can run a script on computers, install their missing Windows updates, or restart them. Scripts are PowerShell or Command, and run as SYSTEM.

  • Several computers: tick them, then click Actions. Shift-click ticks everything in between.
  • One computer: click it. Actions at the top of its panel has the same menu, and its Jobs tab lists what was run on it.
  • Scripts is the library. New adds one while Scripts is open (it's also under New's arrow), and Start from fills in a common one to change. Each script has a time limit: it's stopped when that's up.
  • The built-in domain checks only read: Domain trust (secure channel), Domain controller it uses, Every domain controller, Domain trusts, Account scripts run as, and Domain report with all of them. Domain trust fails the job when the computer's link with the domain is broken.
  • Jobs lists every run. Click one to see each computer, the exit code, what the script wrote there, and exactly what ran.
  • Lock sends everyone signed in back to the sign-in screen with their apps still open. Sign everyone out signs them out straight away. Send a message pops up for whoever is signed in. These and Shut down are for now: a computer offline for more than 15 minutes does not get them later.
  • Restart gives anyone signed in a five minute warning. Install Windows updates doesn't restart: a computer that needs it shows under Needs restart.
  • A computer that's offline gets the job when it's back, for a day. After that the job says Expired.
  • Wake turns on a computer that is off or asleep, with Wake-on-LAN. This server sends it to computers on its own network. For a computer on another network, an online computer there passes it on. Wake-on-LAN has to be on in the BIOS, and in Windows: the computer panel says which, and the built-in script Turn on Wake-on-LAN sets the Windows side.

Confirm it's you. Before anything runs, or a script or workflow is saved, you're asked for a code from your authenticator app. Without MFA it's your password. It's asked again after 10 minutes. Running on more than 10 computers also takes typing how many.

Who can do it. Four permissions under Devices in Roles and people: Restart, wake and update computers, Run scripts, Write scripts, and Manage workflows. Write scripts means being able to run anything on every computer, so give it to few. Every run is in the activity log with who ran it and the script's SHA-256.

To turn all of this off on some computers, set Allow scripts and actions to Disabled in the Group Policy template. Their jobs then say Refused. A computer needs client 1.1.168 or later to take jobs.

A workflow runs steps on computers by itself. Open Workflows under Devices and click New, or pick New workflow from New's arrow.

  • When: a new computer is approved, on a schedule (days and a time, by this server's clock), a computer comes online, is missing Windows updates, needs a restart, or runs low on space on C:.
  • On which computers: every computer, names like SHOP-*, Windows names that contain a word (or don't, such as Server), or only computers with nobody signed in.
  • Steps: run a script from the library, install missing Windows updates, restart, or restart only if Windows needs it. They run in order on each computer and stop at the first one that fails.
  • Run now starts it on every approved computer it's for. Disable keeps it without running it.

Each run is a job under Jobs, by Workflow. A scheduled run reaches a computer that was offline if it's back within four hours. A workflow can only hold steps its maker may run by hand.

The client is a small Windows service with a tray icon. It checks in every minute, says who is signed in, keeps itself on the server's version, and makes the account changes you ask for on a computer's Accounts tab in Devices.

  • Its window: click the tray icon, or open Rabbid Lab Client from the Start menu. It shows whether the computer is connected, to which server, who is signed in to Windows, both versions and the last check-in.
  • When the icon shows: as soon as the client is installed by hand. After a quiet install (Intune, Group Policy) it shows at the next sign-in.
  • No server yet? The window has Connect to a server.
  • Stopping account changes on some computers: in the Group Policy template, set Allow account changes to Disabled.
  • Stopping scripts and actions on some computers: in the same template, set Allow scripts and actions to Disabled.
  • Hiding the client from people: set Hide the client from people at the computer to Enabled, or install with QUIET=1 (the deploy GPO does). No tray icon and no messages; it still connects and waits for approval. Disabled shows the icon even after QUIET=1.

If a computer won't take changes, see A computer that won't take changes.

Once a computer has connected to a server, the client is locked on it: Windows won't uninstall it from Installed apps or from the Rabbid Lab Client window. An administrator at the computer can still stop its service; the lock is on uninstalling. A client that never connected to a server isn't locked.

  1. Open Devices and click the computer. It opens in a panel on the right.
  2. Uninstall now makes the client uninstall itself within a minute. The computer then shows as Offline: click Remove to take it off the list.
  3. Or click Allow for an hour so someone at the computer can uninstall it. Lock again ends that early.

When the computer can't reach the server

  • Uninstall code. Every computer has its own. Click Show code under Uninstall code in the same panel. On the computer, open Rabbid Lab Client, click Uninstall and enter the code. It allows uninstalling for an hour and needs no connection. Showing a code and using one are both in the activity log, and using one is also in the computer's Windows event log.
  • 30 days offline. A computer that hasn't reached this server for 30 days unlocks by itself, so nothing is stuck if the server is gone for good. Turn this off in Settings, Devices.

Before you remove the server

Uninstall the client from the other computers first, with Uninstall now. Once the server and its data are gone, nothing can allow it and the uninstall codes are gone too, so those computers stay locked for the 30 days. Setup's Remove page says how many computers still have the client. The client on the server itself is uninstalled along with it.

When the server is already gone

Remove-RabbidLabClient.ps1, in Tools on Rabbid Lab's GitHub page, clears the lock and uninstalls the client. It needs administrator rights on the computer and writes a line to its Application event log.

  • One computer. Run it there in PowerShell started as administrator.
  • Many that are on. Run it from one computer with -ComputerName and the names. PowerShell remoting has to be on there. The ones that don't answer are listed.
  • Ones that are off. Add it to a new policy in Group Policy Management as a startup script: Computer Configuration, Policies, Windows Settings, Scripts, Startup, PowerShell Scripts. Each computer runs it the next time it starts, and one without the client is left alone.

Delete the Rabbid Lab Client policy first if you deployed the client with Group Policy. It installs the client again wherever it's missing.

A locked computer can't be connected to a different server until it's unlocked. The lock stops the normal ways of removing the client. Someone with administrator rights on the computer who removes it by hand still can, and the computer then shows as Offline.

Rabbid Lab makes every Active Directory change as a dedicated service account, svc-rabbidlab. Its password is long, random and stored encrypted on the server. You set this up once.

Create a new one

  1. Open Users and click Set up. The steps run down the left: Sign in, Account, Review.
  2. Sign in: enter a domain admin username and password. They're only used to create the service account and aren't saved.
  3. Account: name it and pick the OU to create it in, from the list or with Browse….
  4. Review: check it, then click Create service account. Can manage starts as the whole domain; Change limits it to one OU.

The service account gets Full Control over user, group and computer objects there, the same as the Delegation of Control wizard.

Use one you have

Click Use one I have, sign in as the service account, then check it on Review. It needs permission to create and manage users, groups and computers where it works: the whole domain, or the OU you pick with Change.

Not now

Not now on the card turns on read-only for the whole server, after you confirm it's you. Only people whose role can set up the service account can turn it on. They see it, and so does anyone whose role has See it while read-only, in their role's scope: everyone else waits for the service account. The pages read the domain as this server's own computer account, which can read most of it, and nothing can be changed until the service account is set up. Group Policy changes still work with a domain admin login. Turning it on or off goes in the Activity log, and Settings, Active Directory has Turn off read-only.

Picking an OU

Browse… beside any OU list opens the OUs the way Settings lists its pages. An OU with OUs inside has the flask and opens when you click it. Type in Find an OU to search, then click Use this OU, or double-click it.

Change it later

Settings, Active Directory (the gear at the bottom of the views on any Active Directory page opens it) sets the default OU for new users and groups, and has Disconnect. Disconnecting deletes the stored password; the account and its permissions stay in AD until you delete them in Active Directory Users and Computers.

Admin accounts protected by AdminSDHolder (Domain Admins and the like) can be viewed but not changed by the service account.

  1. Open Users and click New in the bar under the banner. Its arrow has New user, New group and New computer on any Active Directory page.
  2. Type the first and last name. The display name and username fill themselves in; change them if you like.
  3. Pick where it goes, and add an email, job title and description if you want.
  4. Leave Make one for me for the password, or pick Type one.
  5. Keep Change it at the first sign-in on, so the person picks their own.
  6. Add groups if you want, then click Make the account.
  7. Copy the password Rabbid Lab shows. It's shown once and not kept.
  1. Open Users, Locked out, or find the person in All users.
  2. Click them to open their card.
  3. Locked out? Click Unlock.
  4. Forgot their password? Click Set a password, pick Make one for me or type one, and keep Change it at the next sign-in on. Setting a password unlocks them too.
  5. Copy the new password and give it to them.

Click a row anywhere in the console (Users, Groups, Computers, Domain health, AD audit, Recycle Bin, Devices, Roles and people) to open it in a panel from the right; click the same row again to close it: its details, its groups (and a group's members), and buttons for the everyday changes. The round › on the panel's edge, or a click on its row again, folds it into a thin strip on the right with the name down it; click the strip, or the row, to open it again. Every row also has a menu with everything that can be changed: open it from ⋯ at the end of the row, or right-click the row.

  1. To change several at once, tick them on the left (Shift-click ticks a run of them), then click Actions in the toolbar. It shows how many are ticked.
  2. Pick a category: Account (enable, disable, unlock, expiration), Password, Groups, Security or Organization (edit properties, rename, move).
  3. Groups have Members (add, remove, copy from or to another group) and Member of. Computers have Account, Groups and Organization.
  4. The menu only lists what applies. With several picked, an item says how many of them it applies to.
  5. The panel's Attributes tab lists every attribute the user, group or computer has in Active Directory, as ADSI Edit shows them. Find narrows it, Show empty adds the ones with no value, and Export saves them as a CSV. Each row's ⋯ (or a right-click) copies it and, with Edit attributes in your role, edits or clears it. Attribute browser opens the same object there.
  6. In the panel, Member of and a group's Members have a tick on each row and Select all above them. Tick some, then Remove beside Add takes them all out at once, after asking when it's more than one. The cross on a row takes out just that one.
  7. Add to groups, Add members, the Remove ones and Copy show everyone there is in a list with a tick each: find to narrow it, tick as many as you need (Tick all shown ticks everything the find left), and the count shows at the bottom. What they are already in, or who is already a member, is left out.

Protected and built-in accounts and groups can be changed too: Active Directory refuses whatever the service account isn't allowed to do. A few things are never offered: the password, sign-in settings and deleting of Rabbid Lab's own account (it would lock itself out), disabling, moving or deleting a domain controller, and deleting a built-in group. Every change goes in the Activity log.

The bar over a list filters it:

  • The search box looks in every column. Pick one column in front of it to look only there, and contains, is or starts with.
  • Each chip after it (Status, Department and so on) lists that column's values with how many rows have each. Tick the ones you want. The numbers follow whatever else is picked.
  • Add/remove filters picks which columns have a chip. A star keeps one at the top of that list.
  • The arrow at the end of the bar opens every chip out side by side, to tick values without opening each one. The magnifier over a column finds a value in it.
  • The save button keeps what is picked under Load filter, in this browser, with a name you give it. The pencil beside a saved filter renames it and the × deletes it. Advanced filter, in the same menu on Users, Groups, Computers and the Devices list, is for what a chip cannot say: older than so many days, does not contain, member of a group, any of the conditions or all of them.
  • Clear takes everything off.

The list has a bar of its own:

  • Actions is for the rows you ticked, and shows how many.
  • Group by gathers the rows under a heading for each value of the column picked beside it, with a count. Click a heading to close or open it. Pick two columns to group inside groups.
  • The two buttons after the count switch between Compact rows (the default) and Relaxed.
  • Click a column name to sort by it, and again to turn the order round. The ⋮ beside it sorts, pins that column to the left or right so it stays put when the list scrolls sideways, groups by it or hides it. Autosize all columns fits every column to what is in it, and Choose columns picks which ones show. Drag the edge of a column name to make it wider or narrower; double-click the edge to fit the column to what is in it.
  • The button at the end of the column names picks what the list shows, such as email, job title, office, manager, OU or password dates. Reset puts it back.

Every table in the console works the same way: Domain health, AD audit, Recycle Bin, Group Policy, the Attribute browser and the lists under Devices too. Drag a heading onto another to move that column. Columns, order, widths, sort and grouping are kept in this browser for each table; Compact or Relaxed is one setting for all of them.

Someone left? Turn their account off first. It can be turned back on; deleting can't be undone.

  1. Open their card in Users.
  2. Click Disable. They show under Disabled.
  3. When you're sure, open it in the side panel again, click Actions, then Delete, and confirm.

Make a group

  1. Open Groups and click New, or pick New group from its arrow.
  2. Name it, pick where it goes, its scope (Global is usual) and its type (Security for permissions, Distribution for email only).
  3. Click Make the group.

Add or take out members

  1. Click the group. Its side panel lists who's in it under Members.
  2. Type in Add a user, group or computer and click who you want.
  3. To take someone out, click the cross beside them.

You can also change a person's groups from their own card in Users.

Empty groups lists groups nobody is in, for tidying up.

Computers lists every computer account in the domain, servers and workstations.

  1. Open Inactive (90+ days) to find computers nobody has used in a while.
  2. Click one and Disable its account. Nobody can sign in to it with a domain account until it's enabled again.
  3. Gone for good? Delete it. The computer would have to join the domain again.

Domain controllers are shown but can't be changed here.

Each Active Directory page has views down the left, with how many are in each. Views that would be empty stay hidden, so the list shows what needs looking at.

  • Users: Locked out, Expired and Expiring passwords (within 14 days), Inactive (90+ days), Privileged accounts, Password never expires, Never signed in, and more.
  • Groups: Security, Distribution, Privileged, Empty groups.
  • Computers: Servers, Workstations, Inactive, Disabled.

Find, at the top of the views, narrows the list. Export saves what's on screen as a CSV for Excel.

Domain health has its own place in the left menu, under Directory. It checks how the domain is set up and what's in it against what Microsoft, PingCastle and Defender for Identity recommend. The checks are grouped into areas: passwords and lockout, privileged accounts, Kerberos and delegation, stale objects, domain and forest, domain controllers, certificates (AD CS), trusts and DNS. Everything is read over LDAP with Rabbid Lab's account, so nothing gets installed on the domain controllers.

  1. Open Not as recommended first. Those are the ones worth fixing.
  2. Click a check to open it in the panel on the right. It shows what it is now, what's recommended and why, and what it found (accounts, computers, groups, templates, trusts or zones).
  3. Click the arrow on something it found to open it in its own page.
  4. Where there's a fix button (Require a password, Disable, Mark sensitive and so on), tick the ones to change and click it. Disabling asks first. The check runs again after.
  5. Under How to fix it is what to do by hand for the rest.
  6. Under Change it, set the new value, sign in as a domain admin (used for that change only, not saved) and click Save. Password and lockout rules are saved in the Default Domain Policy, as Group Policy Management does, and set on the domain straight away. The machine account quota, Guest and the tombstone lifetime are set in Active Directory itself (the tombstone lifetime takes an Enterprise Admin).

"Couldn't be read" means Rabbid Lab's account isn't allowed to see that one, which is normal for fine-grained password policies. "For your notes" isn't a problem by itself, just something to know about. The Source column (under Columns) shows which PingCastle rule or guidance each check comes from.

AD audit lists what changed in Active Directory in the last 30 days, wherever it was done: here, Active Directory Users and Computers, or PowerShell. Sign-ins and other housekeeping are left out, and so is a computer changing its own password.

  1. Pick a view: Last 24 hours, Made, Deleted, Users, and so on.
  2. Click a change to see which attributes changed.

When the change was made in Rabbid Lab, it says who made it. For changes made elsewhere, the domain controllers' security logs have that.

With the AD Recycle Bin on, a deleted user, group, computer or OU can come back as it was, groups and all, for 180 days (by default).

Turn it on (once)

  1. Open Recycle Bin. If it's off, click Turn it on.
  2. Sign in with an Enterprise Admin. It's used for this once and not kept.

It's for the whole forest, and it can't be turned off again. That's how Active Directory works.

Restore something

  1. Find it in Recycle Bin and click it.
  2. Click Restore. It goes back where it was.

Looking in Deleted Objects and restoring take rights only Domain Admins have by default. If Rabbid Lab's account doesn't have them, the page says so.

The Org chart draws who reports to whom from each person's Manager in Active Directory. Each person is a building, managers are the tall ones with their initials on a sign, and each manager's team stands on a platform below them.

  • Drag to move it, scroll to zoom, and Fit shows it all. Expand all and Collapse open and close every team; the pill under a manager does one.
  • Click someone to light up their line to the top and open them in the side panel, with who they report to and who reports to them. Show only this line draws just their line, and Whole organization goes back.
  • Find a person, at the top left, lists who matches by name, username, title or department. Pick one to go to them.
  • By department puts people on a platform for each department. It works with no managers set.
  • The download button saves the chart as a picture (PNG) or a drawing (SVG).

Nobody has a manager set? It shows everyone by department. Set Manager on the Organization tab in Active Directory Users and Computers, and Reporting lines draws itself.

The Attribute browser shows the whole domain as folders, and every attribute of whatever you pick.

  1. Type in the find box over the folders to find any user, group, computer or OU by name, anywhere in the domain. Click one to open it; the matches stay until you clear the box.
  2. Click an OU or container to go into it, and the path at the top to come back out.
  3. Click a user, group or computer to see its attributes. SIDs, GUIDs, dates and flags are shown in words.
  4. Type in Find an attribute or value to narrow the list. Hide empty shows only the attributes with a value; turn it off to see every one the object can have.
  5. Click … on an attribute, or right-click it, to copy it, or to Edit… or Clear value as ADSI Edit does. Editing needs the Edit attributes permission, works inside the service account's scope, and every change goes in the Activity log.
  6. The buttons at the top copy the object's distinguished name and export its attributes to a CSV file.

Passwords, LAPS passwords and BitLocker keys are never shown.

Group Policy lists every GPO in the domain: its status, where it's linked, its WMI filter and when it last changed. The views under Group Policy Objects show the ones not linked anywhere, ones with settings turned off, and ones changed this week.

  1. Click a GPO to open it. Details has its versions in AD and SYSVOL, Links where it applies, Settings what it sets, and Delegation who it applies to and who can change it.
  2. Actions on its panel links it, turns its computer or user settings off, sets its WMI filter, renames, copies, backs it up, restores it, saves its settings report or deletes it.
  3. New (or New GPO under its arrow) makes an empty one, or a copy of another, and can link it straight away. Its settings are then set in the Group Policy editor (gpmc.msc).
  4. On Delegation, type a group, user or computer to make the GPO apply to it. The cross stops it applying there; it can still read the GPO, so computers keep working.
  5. Tick GPOs in the list to link, back up, run gpupdate or delete several at once.

Changes are made as the AD service account. When it isn't allowed, you're asked for a domain admin login. That login is kept in the browser tab for 15 minutes, then forgotten, and never saved on the server. Settings, delegation, new GPOs, copies, deletes and backups need Group Policy Management on the server: Add-WindowsCapability -Online -Name Rsat.GroupPolicy.Management.Tools~~~~0.0.1.0 on Windows 10 or 11, Install-WindowsFeature GPMC on Windows Server.

A GPO does nothing until it's linked to the domain or an OU. It then applies to the users and computers in there, and in the OUs under it.

  • Where linked lists the domain and every OU with the GPOs linked to each. Click one to see them in order.
  • Link order 1 wins: it applies last, over the others. A new link goes at the end, so the ones there already keep winning. Use the … on a link to move it up or down, turn it off, enforce it or remove it.
  • Enforced makes a link apply even where an OU below blocks inheritance, and win over GPOs linked lower down.
  • Block inheritance on an OU stops GPOs linked higher up applying there, unless they're enforced.

A WMI filter limits a GPO to the computers where its query finds something, such as only Windows 11 or only laptops.

  1. Open WMI filters and click New WMI filter. Give it a name and a WQL query.
  2. Try the query first on a few computers under Devices, WMI query: if it finds something there, the filter lets the GPO apply there.
  3. Open a GPO, then Actions, WMI filter… to use it.

A filter that a GPO uses can't be deleted until it's taken off.

Backups are kept on the server, in the same form Group Policy Management uses, under Backups.

  • Back up one GPO from its Actions, several by ticking them, or all of them with Back up all GPOs.
  • Deleting a GPO backs it up first. Restoring a deleted one brings it back with the same ID.
  • Restoring backs up what the GPO has now first. Links aren't part of a backup, so they stay as they are.

Each computer's client reads its Group Policy results every two hours, and right after gpupdate, and sends them with its inventory.

  • Open a computer in Devices and pick its Group Policy tab: when it last refreshed and from which domain controller, any errors, what applied to the computer and to whoever is signed in, what didn't apply and why, and the local policy it ended up with.
  • Devices, Group Policy shows the same for every computer in one list.
  • Run gpupdate applies Group Policy again now: computer policy as SYSTEM, and user policy for whoever is signed in. It's in a computer's Actions menu too, and on a GPO, where it runs on the computers that GPO reaches.

  4. Pick the groups. Tick Administrators for a local admin; Users is ticked for you.
  5. Click Make the account. On a computer in Devices, its client makes it within a second or two.
  6. Copy the password Rabbid Lab shows.

A computer's Accounts tab lists everyone in its Administrators group, domain users and groups too. A domain admin is an administrator of every computer through Domain Admins, even though their account isn't on the computer.

  • Members is the group as Windows lists it. Click a domain group to see who is in it.
  • People opens the groups up and says how each person gets in: added directly, or through which groups.
  • Add puts a local account, or a domain user or group (DOMAIN\name), in the group. The cross takes someone out.
  • The Local admins tab on Devices, beside Computers, shows all of this across every computer at once. Tick rows there, then Actions takes them out of Administrators or deletes local accounts. In a member's panel, the cross on a computer takes them out of just that one.

The people in a domain group come from Active Directory, read as this server's own computer account. Computers send their Administrators group with their inventory, from client 1.1.183 on.

When a computer can't take changes, its Accounts tab says why, and shows who was in Administrators the last time it said.

  • It's offline: the computer is off, or can't reach the server.
  • Its client is older: it updates itself to the server's version within a few minutes.
  • Not listening yet: the client was just started or updated. Give it a minute.
  • Group Policy turns account changes off: Allow account changes is Disabled for that computer.

Everything in Rabbid Lab is allowed by roles. People can have several roles and get everything those roles allow.

  • Administrator is always at the top and allows everything. The server's Windows administrators are always in it.
  • User is always at the bottom, and everyone who signs in has it.

Make a role

  1. Open Roles and people and click New role.
  2. On Display, name it and pick a colour.
  3. On Permissions, switch on what it allows, part by part.
  4. Under Active Directory, Scope limits the role to the OUs you add, and everything inside them. With none, it works everywhere the service account does.
  5. On Members, add who's in it.
  6. Click Save changes.

Roles are in order, highest first: drag them to reorder. People can only change roles below their own highest one. Preview as shows what that role sees.

The order counts in Active Directory too. Nobody can reset the password of, or otherwise change, an account that's in a role above their own, or change a group that puts people in one. A help desk role can't take over an admin that way.

With MFA on, signing in takes your password and then a 6-digit code from an authenticator app, such as Microsoft Authenticator, Google Authenticator or 1Password. It works the same for local accounts, Active Directory accounts and Windows administrators.

Turn it on for yourself

  1. Open Settings, Account and click Set up under Multi-factor authentication.
  2. Scan the QR code with the app, or type the key in.
  3. Type the code the app shows and click Turn on.

Turn off takes a code too.

Require it for a role

In Roles and people, open a role and switch on Require MFA on its Display tab. Everyone in the role who hasn't set it up does that at their next sign-in. Anyone signed in without a code is signed out, you included if you're in the role.

A lost phone

  • Someone with the Reset MFA permission opens the person in Roles and people and clicks Reset MFA. It works on people in roles below their own.
  • If nobody can, run "C:\Program Files\Rabbid Lab\RabbidLab.Server.exe" --reset-mfa username on the server, as an administrator. In Docker: docker exec rabbidlab dotnet RabbidLab.Server.dll --reset-mfa username

The codes come from the time, so the server's clock and the phone's have to agree to within about 30 seconds.

A group in a role counts for everyone in it, people in groups inside it too, the next time they sign in. Their role shows which group it came from, and the group's panel says so. The + beside someone's roles gives them another one, and the cross on a role takes it away.

The dot on each link says whether its site answers: green is up, amber didn't answer just now and is being tried again, red is down. Hover the dot for the detail. Any answer counts as up, a sign-in page included.

The cogwheel on Links, at the right end of the bar under the banner, sets two things:

  • Heartbeat interval: 15 seconds to 5 minutes. A minute to begin with.
  • Show a link as down after no answer for: right away, or 15 seconds to 5 minutes. 30 seconds to begin with, so one slow reply doesn't turn a link red.

Links are checked while the console is open on the Links page. For checks around the clock, with email when something goes down, add a sensor.

The same cogwheel has Export and Import. Export saves every link with its icon, the groups in their order and these settings as one file. A sensor checks one thing on a timer, from this server, and the Sensors page shows what's down.

  1. Open Sensors and click Add a sensor.
  2. Pick the check:
    • Ping: the machine answers.
    • Port: a port is open, such as 445 for file shares, 636 for LDAPS or 3389 for remote desktop.
    • Web page: the page answers. A sign-in page counts as up; an error 500 or higher counts as down.
    • DNS lookup: a name resolves, on the DNS server you name or the one this server uses.
    • Certificate: warns when a site's certificate has under 21 days left, and is down once it expires.
  3. Type the address. Check now tries it before you save.
  4. Pick a group, how often to check, and when it alerts, then click Add the sensor.

A sensor is down after two misses in a row, so one lost reply isn't an outage; change it with Down after. Its alerts go to the channels ticked under Send to: email, Discord, Teams, Slack, ntfy or any webhook, set up once in Settings, Notifications. Click a sensor for its history, to pause it, or to change it.

  • Cards and Grid, at the right of the row above the sensors, switch between the two. Your browser remembers which.
  • The Rows view is a table like every other one: click a heading to sort, drag a heading onto another to move it, drag its right edge to resize, and Columns picks what shows and has Reset.
  • Groups work as they do on Links: drag a card onto another group to move it, New group in the bar under the banner, the filter button to show one group or only the sensors that are down, and hover a group's name to drag, rename or delete it.

Each sensor says when it alerts, under Alerts when you add or change it:

  • Alert when it has been down for: right away, 1 to 30 minutes, an hour, or never (it only shows on the page).
  • While it stays down: once, or again every hour, 6 hours or day.
  • When it comes back: an all-clear, only after a down alert went.
  • Depends on: while that sensor is down, this one waits instead of alerting, and shows as Waiting. If it's still down once the other is back, it alerts then.
  • Send to: email and the channels set up in Settings, Notifications. A kind that isn't set up yet can be set up from there.

New sensors start with what's set in Settings, Sensors. Use these alerts on other sensors copies one sensor's alerts to others; Use on every sensor in Settings does it for all of them. Something that comes back before its time is up alerts nobody.

On a sensor, Send a test alert sends its alert, marked as a test, to its channels. Pause stops it for 15 minutes, an hour, 4 hours, until 8 tomorrow or until you start it again, and can take what depends on it along. Gather alerts in Settings, Sensors sends one alert when several go down close together.

On Links, Watch it in a link's dialog gives it a sensor that checks it every minute and alerts the same way. Its dot comes from that sensor, and a small eye shows it's watched. Watch links in the bar above the links ticks many at once: tick them (or Tick all in a group), pick when to alert and where, and click Watch. Untick one to stop watching it.

Set up email under Settings, Notifications, with Edit on the Email row:

  • Gmail: your address and an app password (Google Account, Security, 2-Step Verification, App passwords).
  • Microsoft 365: an app registration in Microsoft Entra with the Mail.Send application permission and admin consent. Type the tenant, the application (client) ID and a client secret. From has to be a mailbox in the tenant.
  • Another mail server: any server or relay that takes STARTTLS on port 587.

Send a test email saves the settings and sends one to everyone on the list. Test on any channel's row sends it one. Passwords and secrets are stored encrypted on this server.

Settings, Console address lists where to open Rabbid Lab: this computer's names and IP addresses, and any you add. It also shows the HTTPS certificate, the port and the firewall rule.

  • Add an address gives the console a name of your own, such as lab. On a computer in a domain it can add an alias (CNAME) for it in DNS on a domain controller, with an account you type for that. The account needs DNS admin rights, and it isn't saved. It never changes a record that's already there, or takes a computer's name.
  • Not in a domain? Add the name on whatever answers DNS for your network, such as your router or Pi-hole. The dialog shows the record to add.
  • Added addresses get certificates of their own. The console's own certificate doesn't change, and computers in Devices connect through any of the addresses. In a domain, an address in the domain is signed by a certificate authority made for this install, which can sign only for names in that domain. To open it from other computers without a warning, Download the authority on the page and add it to Trusted Root Certification Authorities there, or run the certutil line shown to put it on every domain computer. Short names, names outside the domain and installs with no domain get a self-signed certificate, trusted on this computer.
  • Port 443, under Port and firewall, makes the console answer there as well, so https://lab.home.lan opens it with no port typed. Its own port keeps working, and computers in Devices keep using it. Turning it on or off restarts Rabbid Lab, which takes a few seconds, and adds or removes a firewall rule if Setup made the console's. If another program on the server already has port 443, the page says so and nothing else changes.
  • Remove takes an address off, and its alias out of DNS if Rabbid Lab added it. Removing Rabbid Lab with everything takes the aliases out too. Setup's host name can be removed the same way, with the alias Setup added. This computer's own name and IP addresses only come off the list, since the console still answers there: Show them at the bottom puts them back.

The i at the top right gathers what someone helping you needs to know: the version, the machine, how it's installed, the sign-in domain, and how many devices, links and sensors there are. Click Copy and paste it into your message. It has no passwords in it, and only shows what your roles let you see.

Rabbid Lab checks GitHub for a new version on start, every 12 hours, and when you click Check for updates.

  • On Windows, a new version installs automatically once nobody has used Rabbid Lab for a couple of minutes. Turn that off with Update automatically under Settings, Updates.
  • Install now installs straight away. Rabbid Lab is stopped for under a minute while it installs, and every open page shows the same progress.
  • Only updates signed with Rabbid Lab's release key install.
  • Computers in Devices update their client to the server's version by themselves.

In Docker, update with docker compose up -d --build.

Settings, Activity log lists every sign-in and change, who made it, and when. That covers accounts made, passwords set, groups changed, computers approved and updates installed. Passwords are never in it.

  2. Run "C:\Program Files\Rabbid Lab\RabbidLab.Server.exe" --add-admin admin

In Docker: docker exec rabbidlab dotnet RabbidLab.Server.dll --add-admin admin

Everything is in one data folder: C:\ProgramData\Rabbid Lab on Windows, /data in Docker. Back it up to keep everything.

  • accounts.json: how people sign in, and Rabbid Lab's own accounts.
  • roles.json: roles, what they allow, and who's in them.
  • sessions.json: who's signed in, encrypted.
  • clients.json: the computers in Devices.
  • directory-tools.json: Rabbid Lab's AD account, its password encrypted.
  • links.json: the Links page.
  • activity.jsonl: the activity log.
  • Keys: the keys the encrypted parts use. Without them, the encrypted parts can't be read on another machine.
